Stereoisomerism and Chirality in Octahedral Complexes

In coordination chemistry, identical molecular formulas can give rise to distinct constitutional isomers or spatial stereoisomers. This module examines the structural distinction between constitutional isomers and stereoisomers, and demonstrates how point-group symmetry governs chirality in six-coordinate octahedral complexes.

Unit 1

Taxonomy of Isomerism: Structural Isomers vs. Chiral Isomers

Isomers are compounds with identical elemental formulas but distinct atomic arrangements. In coordination chemistry, they are divided into two primary classes: Structural (Constitutional) Isomers, which differ in bonding connectivity, and Stereoisomers (including geometric diastereomers and chiral enantiomers), which share identical bond connectivity but differ in spatial orientation.

1. Structural (Constitutional) Isomers

Possess different atom-to-atom connectivity or different ligands within the primary coordination sphere:

  • Ionization Isomers: Exchange of a ligand inside the inner coordination sphere with a counter-ion in the outer sphere, e.g., [Co(NH3)5Br]SO4 vs. [Co(NH3)5(SO4)]Br.
  • Linkage Isomers: Ambidentate ligands coordinating through different donor atoms, e.g., nitro (M–NO2) vs. nitrito (M–ONO).
  • Coordination Isomers: Distribution exchange of ligands between cationic and anionic complex ions, e.g., [Co(NH3)6][Cr(CN)6] vs. [Cr(NH3)6][Co(CN)6].
  • Hydrate (Solvate) Isomers: Differ in whether water molecules act as inner-sphere ligands or lattice water.

2. Stereoisomers & Chiral Isomers (Enantiomers)

Maintain identical metal-ligand bonding sequences, differing exclusively in 3D spatial arrangement:

  • Geometric Isomers (Diastereomers): Non-mirror-image stereoisomers where relative trans/cis (90° vs. 180°) bond angles differ, such as cis/trans or fac/mer. They exhibit distinct physical and spectroscopic properties.
  • Chiral (Optical) Isomers (Enantiomers): Stereoisomers that are non-superimposable mirror images of one another. Unlike structural or geometric isomers, a pair of enantiomers shares identical scalar physical properties (melting point, dipole moment, NMR chemical shifts) in an achiral environment, differing only in their interaction with plane-polarized light or other chiral species.

Concept Check: Structural Connectivity vs. Spatial Handedness

Consider two complexes: Pair A consists of [Co(en)2(NO2)2]Cl and [Co(en)2(NO2)Cl](NO2). Pair B consists of Δ-cis-[Co(en)2Cl2]+ and Λ-cis-[Co(en)2Cl2]+. How do their physical separation methods differ?

Pair A (Ionization / Structural Isomers): Have entirely different chemical bonds in the inner sphere. Adding AgNO3(aq) immediately precipitates AgCl(s) from the first complex, whereas the second has chloride bound covalently to Cobalt(III).

Pair B (Chiral Enantiomers): Possess identical Co–N and Co–Cl connectivity and identical bond angles. They cannot be separated by standard achiral chromatography or precipitation; resolution requires a chiral resolving agent (e.g., forming diastereomeric salts with optically pure tartrate).

Unit 2

Symmetry Criteria for Chiral Complexes

Unlike tetrahedral carbon compounds that typically require an asymmetric atom bonded to four different groups, octahedral complexes frequently exhibit chirality through overall molecular dissymmetry.

Improper Rotation Axis (Sn) Criterion

A molecule is chiral if and only if it lacks any improper axis of rotation (Sn):

  • Mirror Plane (σ ≡ S1): If a plane divides the complex into two halves that are reflections of each other, the complex is achiral.
  • Inversion Center (i ≡ S2): If every atom at (x, y, z) has an identical atom at (−x, −y, −z), the complex is achiral.
  • Proper Rotation (Cn): The presence of proper rotation axes does not prevent chirality. Many classic chiral chelates belong to chiral point groups C1, C2, or D3.
